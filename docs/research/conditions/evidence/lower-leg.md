## Interpretation and access conventions

- **Evidence** describes the population, intervention and finding actually examined. **App inference** describes a proposed translation that needs clinical review; it is not a study finding.
- A trial exclusion limits generalisability; it does not establish a contraindication. A helpful exercise does not establish that other exercise is harmful. Symptom provocation, structural injury risk and absence of evidence are different concepts.
- Doses below are studied protocols, not default prescriptions. Repetition maximum, percentage maximum voluntary contraction and pain-monitoring thresholds must not become user-chosen starting loads or universal progression rules.
- “Full text” means the article body/methods or tables were retrieved and inspected, sometimes via the legitimate Europe PMC XML service. A PMC challenge and a successful XML retrieval are stated separately. Public nonpublisher copies are identified rather than represented as publisher access.
- Each identifier and URL below was checked in this session. Where only DOI and/or PMID was verified, other identifiers are deliberately omitted. No trial registration, protocol-only paper or abstract is represented as a completed treatment trial.

## 1. Midportion Achilles tendinopathy

### Sources and substantive extraction

**ACH-G: Chimenti et al., final 2024 clinical practice guideline.** [Publisher final article](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2024.0302); [AOPT final PDF](https://orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/chimenti_et_al_2024_achilles_pain_stiffness_and_muscle_power_deficits_midportion_achilles_tendinopathy_revision_2024.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39611662/). DOI **10.2519/jospt.2024.0302**, PMID **39611662**. Final publication: December 2024, 54(12):CPG1–CPG32; full guideline text retrieved through indexed publisher/AOPT content, not the public-comment draft. Scope is **midportion**, not insertional disease. Strong recommendation: progressive tendon loading with loads as tolerated in people without presumed tendon frailty. The **final** frequency recommendation is **at least three times weekly**, at an intensity as tolerated; its frequency recommendation is expert opinion, not proof that an exact weekly threshold is optimal. Complete rest is discouraged; activities can continue within pain tolerance. Stretching applies to a dorsiflexion deficit rather than every patient. Loading may be eccentric, concentric, isometric, isotonic or plyometric; comparative evidence does not establish one universally superior method. Temporary heel lifts can reduce dorsiflexion during activity. This is a guideline synthesis with no single treatment dose, comparator, follow-up or harms denominator.

**ACH-R1: Beyer et al., 2015 RCT.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018970/); DOI **10.1177/0363546515584760**, PMID **26018970**. [Public nonpublisher copy of primary paper](https://tripp.co.za/wp-content/uploads/Beyer2015_Achillestendinopathy.pdf) inspected, including methods and attrition; publisher DOI access failed. Fifty-eight recreational athletes with unilateral chronic midportion symptoms >3 months were randomised. Twelve-week heavy slow resistance (HSR) used three bilateral calf exercises, three sessions/week, 3-second concentric/3-second eccentric movement, 2–3-minute inter-set rests. Progression: 3×15RM week 1; 3×12RM weeks 2–3; 4×10RM weeks 4–5; 4×8RM weeks 6–8; 4×6RM weeks 9–12. Comparator: straight- and bent-knee unilateral eccentric heel lowers, each 3×15, twice daily, seven days/week. Both improved VISA-A/pain at 12 and 52 weeks without between-group superiority; HSR adherence was higher. One eccentric-group participant had a partial rupture while playing badminton; HSR withdrawals included ankle and back pain. Causality was not established. The full-ROM protocol cannot be transferred to insertional disease. Small sample, attrition, no untreated control.

**ACH-R2: Silbernagel et al., 2007 RCT.** [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17307888/); [publisher](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0363546506298279). DOI **10.1177/0363546506298279**, PMID **17307888**. Abstract inspected; full methods not successfully retrieved from the publisher in this pass. Thirty-eight patients received the same progressive rehabilitation; 19 continued running/jumping under a pain-monitoring model and 19 stopped those activities for the first six weeks. No significant difference in improvement; both improved through 12 months. The abstract supports that compulsory cessation was not superior in this small monitored sample. It does not establish unrestricted sport safety, an app-wide numeric pain threshold, or equivalence for rupture, postoperative disease or all anatomical subtypes. Exact loading sets and adverse-event ascertainment remain unverified from full text.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Supportive candidates:** straight-/bent-knee progressive calf loading, with HSR and eccentric execution identities kept distinct where equipment/range differ (ACH-G, ACH-R1). Stretching remains flexibility work and earns no strength-set credit.
- **Ordinary training:** symptom-guided adjustment of calf loading and impact is defensible; a blanket ban on all calf raises, running, squats or dorsiflexion is not established (ACH-G, ACH-R2). The app cannot infer “no tendon frailty” from a user selection.
- **Unresolved:** individual starting load, acceptable pain response, loading stage and need for clinician assessment. No automatic jump progression or universal “add five seconds” rule follows from these sources.

## 2. Insertional Achilles tendinopathy

**IAT-R: Pringels et al., 2025 RCT.** [Primary full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12171478/); [publisher](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/59/9/640). DOI **10.1136/bjsports-2024-109138**, PMID **40011018**. Full methods/results inspected. Forty-two sport-active adults with symptoms >3 months received 24-week low- versus high-compression rehabilitation with weekly physiotherapist supervision. Low compression limited dorsiflexion, omitted calf stretching and added 12-mm heel lifts; high compression used end-range dorsiflexion/stretching. Both used staged loading: isometrics 5×30s at 50% MVC progressing to 5×60s at 70%; isotonic 4×15 at 65% 1RM to 4×6 at 85%; energy-storage 3×10 at 75% to 3×6 at 85%; later running/jumping every 2–3 days. Stages required at least two weeks, pain <5/10 during/one hour after/next morning, and no weekly deterioration. VISA-A between-group differences favored low compression: 12.9 points (95% CI 6.2–19.6) at 12 weeks; 10.4 (3.7–17.1) at 24. No reported adverse events. The bundled comparison cannot isolate heel lifts, range limitation or stretching individually; small athletic sample and exclusions limit transferability.

**IAT-S: Ko et al., 2023 network meta-analysis.** [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9903592/); [Europe PMC full-text XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9903592/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1186/s12891-023-06170-x**, PMID **36750789**. PMC body initially opened, later requests challenged; full XML methods/conclusions inspected. Evaluated nonsurgical insertional treatment trials for short-term pain; eccentric exercise plus soft-tissue therapy ranked well, but certainty was low/very low and the review explicitly could not recommend a best treatment. Heterogeneous small trials, combinations and short follow-up prevent ranking from becoming a fixed exercise prescription. This predates IAT-R and should not override that newer direct comparison. Harms and optimal dose were not established sufficiently for an app protocol.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Supportive candidate:** controlled calf loading with an explicit dorsiflexion-limited execution variant; keep separate from a heel drop below a step (IAT-R). Bodyweight versus externally loaded, seated versus standing and isometric versus isotonic require separate execution/measurement treatment.
- **Proposed modification, clinical review required:** preferentially limit loaded end-range dorsiflexion and omit provocative calf stretching in early rehabilitation, based on the low-compression *package*. Do not assert that any dorsiflexion or any stretch causes structural harm.
- **Unresolved:** exact neutral limit for an individual, heel-lift use/withdrawal and clinician-managed stage. The RCT restricted tendon-loading sport in stages 1–3; this does not justify a permanent sport ban. ACH-G is not an insertional guideline.

## 3. Plantar heel pain compatible with plantar fasciitis

**PHP-G: Koc et al., final 2023 guideline.** [Publisher](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2023.0303); [AOPT PDF](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/koc_et_al_2023_heel_pain_plantar_fasciitis_revision_2023.pdf). DOI **10.2519/jospt.2023.0303**. Full indexed guideline recommendations/evidence synthesis inspected; a later publisher request returned 403. Recommends plantar-fascia-specific and gastrocnemius/soleus stretching, and foot/ankle resistance training. Evidence supports combinations with education/manual therapy, but does not identify a superior strength modality or optimal dose/timing. Stretching studies reported transient mild/moderate pain; no serious adverse events reported in the stretching evidence synthesis. The guideline is for clinically compatible nonarthritic heel pain, not a diagnosis of every painful heel. It supplies no evidence that all loaded plantar flexion, toe extension or running must be excluded.

**PHP-R: Rathleff et al., 2015 RCT.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25145882/); [publisher record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sms.12313); [public nonpublisher primary-paper PDF](https://braceworks.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Rathleff-og-M%C3%B8lgaard-2014_High-load-strength-training-improves-outcome-in-patients-with-plantar-fasciitis-A-randomized-controlled-trial-with-12-month-follow-up.pdf). DOI **10.1111/sms.12313**, PMID **25145882**. Full PDF methods/results inspected. Forty-eight adults, ultrasound-verified disease and heel pain ≥3 months; both groups received gel heel inserts/education. Strength: unilateral heel raise on a step with towel beneath toes, every other day for three months, 3s up/2s top/3s down; 3×12RM initially, 4×10RM after two weeks, 5×8RM after four weeks; bilateral start if required. Stretch comparator: seated plantar-fascia stretch 10×10s, three times/day. FFI favored strength by 29 points (95% CI 6–52) at three months; no between-group difference at 1, 6 or 12 months. Primary follow-up 81%. Minor delayed muscle soreness was reported; no other adverse events. This supports faster short-term improvement, not durable strength superiority or an obligatory toe-extension exercise for every heel-pain presentation.

**PHP-S: Morrissey et al., 2021 best-practice guide.** [Full publisher article](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2021/03/30/bjsports-2019-101970); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33785535/). DOI **10.1136/bjsports-2019-101970**, PMID **33785535**. Full indexed synthesis and education sections inspected. Combined systematic review, expert reasoning and patient values: core treatment was fascia stretching, taping and individual education; escalating care for insufficient response. Advice to break up prolonged standing and adjust aggravating running reflects the combined guide/expert reasoning, not a trial proving harm at a particular duration. No universal step count, standing limit or running dose was established. Harms were not quantified for the education component.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Supportive candidates:** fascia-specific stretch; knee-straight and knee-bent calf stretches; progressive calf/foot resistance. PHP-R's towel-under-toes heel raise is a distinct variant, with potential conflict with a first-MTP extension limit.
- **Ordinary training inference:** reduce or substitute activities that reproduce/worsen the user's heel symptoms; avoid claiming a specific universal forbidden movement. The RCT intentionally loaded the plantar fascia, so “avoid all plantar-fascia loading” would contradict the treatment evidence.
- **Unresolved:** diagnostic alternatives (including bone stress injury), individual pain policy, dose in acute/atypical symptoms, and how other restrictions alter a candidate execution.

## 4. Lateral ankle sprain

**ANK-G: Martin et al., 2021 clinical practice guideline (shared source with section 5).** [Publisher](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2021.0302); [AOPT full PDF](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/jospt.2021.0302.pdf). DOI **10.2519/jospt.2021.0302**. Full indexed recommendations inspected. Acute/postacute LAS: external support and progressively tolerated weight bearing, selected for severity/healing/pain; severe injuries may require immobilisation for up to ten days. Structured exercise may include protected active ROM, stretching, neuromuscular/postural/balance work, selected for impairments and severity. These are clinician decisions rather than a universal immediate exercise routine. For **chronic instability**, recommends proprioceptive/neuromuscular exercise and advises against bracing/taping alone to improve postural stability. The guideline's acute and chronic populations must remain distinct. No single set/rep prescription or trial harms rate applies to this guideline.

**LAS-R1: Bleakley et al., 2010 RCT.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20457737/); [publisher](https://www.bmj.com/content/340/bmj.c1964); DOI **10.1136/bmj.c1964**, PMID **20457737**. PubMed abstract inspected; publisher body returned 403. N=101 with acute grade I/II sprain. Early exercise during the first week versus protection/rest/ice/compression/elevation improved LEFS at weeks 1 and 2 (adjusted differences 5.28 and 4.92); no other pain/swelling differences. Reinjury at 16 weeks was 4%, two per arm. Exact exercise repetitions and session duration not verified from full methods. This is evidence for selected mild/moderate assessed injuries, not immediate loading after an undifferentiated injury or grade III protocol.

**LAS-R2: Brison et al., 2016 RCT, important contrary finding.** [Full publisher article](https://www.bmj.com/content/355/bmj.i5650.long); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5112179/). DOI **10.1136/bmj.i5650**. Full methods/results inspected. N=503, age ≥16, simple grade I/II acute sprains; usual care (including graduated weight bearing) versus added supervised physiotherapy: initial assessment plus up to seven 30-minute sessions, stage-based ROM/isometric/band/bodyweight home exercises. Excellent FAOS recovery at three months: 43% versus 37%, absolute difference 6% (95% CI −3% to 15%); no clinically important benefit through six months. Reinjury 7.5% versus 8.4%. No separate comprehensive adverse-event extraction verified. This finding limits assumptions that more supervised sessions invariably improve outcomes; it does not establish exercise is harmful or unnecessary for every sprain.

**LAS-S: Wagemans et al., 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis.** [Primary review full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8824326/); DOI **10.1371/journal.pone.0262023**, PMID **35134061**. Full text inspected. Fourteen RCTs; reported total varies between abstract (2182) and body (2184), so do not silently resolve the discrepancy. Exercise-based rehabilitation reduced recurrent sprain prevalence versus usual care; abstract OR 0.60 (95% CI 0.36–0.99). Sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk exercise content lost statistical significance. Programs varied from 1–12 weeks, 5–84 sessions; optimal content, volume and intensity remain uncertain; patient-reported and clinical outcomes were inconsistent. No single safe progression or adverse-event rate can be deduced from pooled benefit.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Supportive candidates after assessment:** protected ankle active ROM, progressive ankle/calf resistance, supported balance progressing to more demanding control (ANK-G). No automatic acute protocol is justified by these varied programs.
- **Ordinary training inference:** require injury severity/healing/weight-bearing guidance before selecting unstable, impact, cutting or loaded end-range work. This is a conservative scope gate, not evidence that all such activity is forever contraindicated.
- **Restriction-only possibility:** unassessed recent injury, suspected fracture or clinician-directed immobilisation should not generate a self-directed rehabilitation workout. Do not infer an acute fracture is absent because “sprain” was selected.

## 5. Chronic ankle instability

**CAI-R1: Hall et al., 2018 RCT, clinical outcomes part I.** [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6089027/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29975573/). DOI **10.4085/1062-6050-385-16**, PMID **29975573**. Full PMC protocol/table inspected; Europe PMC XML failed (500). Thirty-nine young adults with questionnaire-defined CAI; 20-minute supervised sessions, three/week for six weeks: balance versus strength versus mild cycling control. Balance included progressive single-leg stance and hop-to-stabilisation/reach, advancing only after error-free performance. Strength combined band dorsiflexion/inversion/eversion, heel raises and clinician manual PNF. Band sets were 3×10 then 4×10 each fortnight with increased resistance; heel raises/PNF progressed 2×10 to 4×15. Both active programs improved balance, strength and side-hop performance at six weeks; strength also improved eccentric eversion. One participant could not progress balance training and was excluded. No long-term recurrence endpoint or systematic adverse-event result was established in this extraction. Small, supervised multicomponent trial; isolated single-leg stance cannot inherit the whole bundle's effects.

**CAI-R2: Hall et al., 2015 RCT.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25365134/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4299733/). DOI **10.4085/1062-6050-49.3.71**, PMID **25365134**. Abstract inspected; Europe PMC XML returned 500. N=39, mostly college-age CAI; resistance-band or PNF strength versus no rehabilitation, three/week for six weeks. Strength and perceived instability improved; Y-Balance and crossover-hop performance did **not** improve. Exact resistance table/harms not independently extracted from full text. This contrasts with CAI-R1's multicomponent protocol; strength gains are not synonymous with restoration of every balance/function deficit.

**CAI-S: 2024 systematic review of balance training.** [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10860262/); [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10860262/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1186/s13643-024-02455-x**, PMID **38347564**. Full XML abstract and trial-characteristics/quality tables inspected. Nine RCTs, 341 participants; table mean ages approximately 16–34 despite the abstract calling CAI an older-patient problem. Programs varied: 4–6 weeks, 5–20 minutes/session when reported, 2–5 days/week. Balance versus blank control improved CAIT (MD 3.95, 95% CI 3.26–4.64) and selected SEBT directions; compared with strength, CAIT/FAAM-ADL favored balance but dynamic-stability differences were not established. Small heterogeneous trials with limited blinding; no universal dose or specific harm estimate follows. This corroborates ANK-G while retaining modality and population differences.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Supportive candidates:** supported single-leg balance; separately identified band ankle directions; calf raises as appropriate. Hopping is a later tested component, not the starting exercise for every person with instability (ANK-G, CAI-R1).
- **Ordinary training:** instability history alone does not establish a permanent prohibition on unilateral stance, inversion/eversion or impact. Use control/capacity/stage to choose progressions; bracing alone does not substitute for exercise.
- **Unresolved:** safe home unsupervised progression, return-to-sport readiness and benefits beyond the young supervised samples. A general “avoid unstable surfaces” rule would need to distinguish an uncontrolled task from deliberately progressed rehabilitation.

## 6. Posterior tibial tendinopathy

**PTT-S: Ross et al., 2018 systematic review of RCTs/guidelines.** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6157513/); [Europe PMC full-text XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6157513/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1136/bmjsem-2018-000430**. PMC challenge; full XML tables/methods inspected. Three small trials, 93 enrolled participants, mostly women in their 50s with stage I/II dysfunction. Supports possible added eccentric strengthening benefit with orthoses/stretching, but sparse studies and poor exercise reporting reduce certainty. Table reports Kulig's plantarflexed adduction protocol: 3×15 at 15RM, twice daily, 12 weeks, 5-second contraction, 1–2-minute rests; concentric neutral-to-end-range versus eccentric reverse. Houck used bilateral/single-leg heel raises and band adduction/inversion in plantarflexion, 2–3×10 progressing toward 30, twice daily, 12 weeks. These are review-extracted protocol details, **not independently verified primary full-text doses**. Findings do not generalise to rigid advanced deformity; diagnostic stage matters. No reliable pooled harms estimate or universally optimal dose.

**PTT-R1: Kulig et al., 2009 RCT.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19022863/); [publisher PDF attempted](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-pdf/89/1/26/31662282/ptj0026.pdf). DOI **10.2522/ptj.20070242**, PMID **19022863**. Abstract accessed; publisher full text failed through redirect/500. N=36 adults with stage I/II symptoms, 12-week orthoses/stretching alone versus plus concentric or eccentric progressive resistance. FFI improved in all; eccentric arm improved most; post-walk pain improved across groups. Exact resistance details above remain indirect from PTT-S. There is no untreated comparator and no proof that strengthening without orthoses is equivalent. Adverse-event reporting not available from the abstract.

**PTT-R2: Houck et al., 2015 RCT, contrary/limiting result.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25857939/). DOI **10.1177/1071100715579906**, PMID **25857939**. Abstract inspected. Stage II participants received orthosis/stretching versus orthosis/stretching plus moderate-intensity home strengthening over 12 weeks; 36 contributed treatment-group data in PTT-S. Both improved pain/function; between-group differences were minimal and deep posterior-compartment isometric strength did not differ. This tempers claims that any added home strengthening program provides a large effect. Full primary exercise methods and harms not retrieved.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Supportive candidate:** posterior-tibialis-focused resisted adduction/inversion in plantarflexion; heel-raise progression may be useful within a clinician-selected early-stage program. Generic inversion with arbitrary range is not automatically equivalent to the tested execution.
- **Ordinary training inference:** modify painful medial-ankle loading and respect prescribed support/weight-bearing limits. No trial establishes a universal ban on pronation, all running, calf raises or flat-foot weight bearing.
- **Unresolved:** stage, flexible versus fixed deformity, appropriate support, load and isolated exercise benefit. Advanced/progressive deformity is outside these early-stage trials; evidence absence must not be converted into a contraindication.

## 7. Peroneal tendinopathy

**PER-G: van Dijk et al., ESSKA-AFAS 2018 consensus.** [Full publisher article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00167-018-4971-x); [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6154028/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1007/s00167-018-4971-x**, PMID **29767272**. Full text and reference trail inspected; publisher notes a correction at DOI **10.1007/s00167-018-5021-4** (not separately assessed here). Consensus distinguishes tendinopathy, tear/rupture and subluxation/dislocation. For acute uncomplicated tendon symptoms, weight bearing once painless, then ROM/tendon loading; panel generally avoided immobilisation. Evidence was explicitly limited. Crucially, cited conservative references include a mixed-tendon PRP pilot and studies of **peroneal luxation/subluxation**, not a randomised exercise comparison in isolated tendinopathy. This cannot supply a validated eversion dose, fixed inversion limit or permanent running ban. Postoperative retinacular-repair restrictions belong to that surgical population, not all peroneal pain. No quantified nonsurgical exercise harms estimate or comparative dose.

**PER-R1: Hensley & Kavchak, 2012 case report (online 2011).** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21570893/). DOI **10.1016/j.math.2011.04.004**, PMID **21570893**. Abstract inspected, primary full treatment table not retrieved. One 50-year-old woman with chronic lateral ankle pain consistent with peroneal tendinopathy improved over eight visits using manual therapy and a home program; improvement followed adding a lateral calcaneal glide after earlier orthotics/strengthening/manual approaches had not succeeded. Returned to work/recreational exercise. No comparator, precise isolated exercise dose or reliable harms estimate; no causal attribution to eversion strengthening alone.

**PER-R2: 2025 peroneal tenography service evaluation.** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12051077/); [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12051077/fullTextXML). DOI **10.7759/cureus.81742**, PMID **40330353**. PMC challenge, full XML methods/abstract inspected. Retrospective 27-patient cohort, mixed tendinosis/tears; median age 50; 12-month follow-up after final management. Care included brief boot/physiotherapy, then band peroneal strengthening for selected symptoms before tenography/escalation. Fifteen reported adequate relief after tenography. This is **not an exercise effectiveness study**; exercise dose and isolated harms were not established. Its boot use also illustrates that practice varies by diagnosis and cannot replace PER-G with one automatic treatment.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Disposition candidate:** restrictions/modifications and research gap, with supportive resisted eversion only as a clinically reviewed candidate, not a validated condition-specific prescription.
- **App inference:** avoid repeatedly provoking lateral-ankle symptoms; assess snapping, loss of function or suspected tear/instability before loading. A universal prohibition on eversion would conflict with tendon-loading rationale; a universal inversion ban is not proven by these studies.
- **Unresolved:** diagnosis, range, intensity, repetition/frequency and progression. Do not borrow the Achilles HSR dose and call it peroneal trial evidence. Targeted searches found consensus, case reports and mixed-pathology clinical series rather than a verified isolated-tendinopathy exercise RCT; this is a search limitation, not proof that none exists.

## 8. Medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS)

**MTSS-R1: Moen et al., 2012 RCT.** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3352296/); [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3352296/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1186/1758-2555-4-12**. PMC challenge; full XML methods/tables inspected. Seventy-four athletes >16, symptoms >3 weeks and diffuse posteromedial tenderness ≥5cm; suspected stress fracture/compartment syndrome excluded. All used graded running three times/week with a rest day between sessions; arms added calf stretch/strength five times/week or compression stockings. Running was withheld when walking hurt; initiation followed two pain-free walking days. Trial advancement required pain <4/10 during running; pain ≥4 afterwards/next day prevented advancement and reduced running time. Days to finish: 105.2 running alone, 117.6 added exercise, 102.1 compression; no significant differences or satisfaction benefit. Fourteen withdrew for inadequate progress. No untreated/rest comparator or strong harms evidence. These trial-specific pain rules must not be exported to bone stress fracture care.

**MTSS-R2: Naderi et al., 2025 RCT.** [Full PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11826869/); [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11826869/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1177/23259671241311849**, PMID **39958697**. Full XML methods/results inspected after intermittent PMC challenges. Forty recreational runners, mean age 23.9, diagnosed MTSS; 12 weeks. Both groups received multimodal care including graded walk/run, ice and five shockwave sessions. Added program: short foot 5s holds for up to three minutes; band inversion/plantarflexion 3×12–15; toe curls 3×15–20; calf stretches 5×20–30s; foam rolling 4×45s. Progression increased sets/load after symptom-stable performance. Exercise-session frequency was not clearly established in the inspected intervention section and is **unresolved**. No added improvement in pain or MTSS severity at 6/12 weeks; QoL and foot-posture measures improved. No side effects reported. Bundled care, small sample and no long-term recurrence endpoint; posture change is not proven prevention of fracture.

**MTSS-S: 2026 current systematic review.** [Publisher abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1466853X26000830). DOI **10.1016/j.ptsp.2026.101962**. Abstract and indexed introduction inspected; full methods/tables not retrieved. Three randomised runner trials (130 participants), searches through June 2025. No clearly superior noninvasive treatment; exercise additions did not outperform comparators for pain/function; arch-support orthoses with multimodal care offered short-term pain benefit not sustained. Heterogeneity and few trials limit certainty. Do not confuse this treatment review with [2025 prevention meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40633262/) (DOI **10.1016/j.gaitpost.2025.07.312**): prevention results in uninjured populations do not establish rehabilitation efficacy.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Disposition candidate:** symptom-guided loading modification, with exercise options marked uncertain for MTSS-specific symptom benefit. Neither direct trial supports promising that calf strengthening shortens recovery.
- **Ordinary training inference:** reduce provocative impact volume rather than mandate permanent running exclusion. Obtain assessment for focal bony pain or atypical progression before treating it as MTSS. MTSS trial screening is not an app diagnostic algorithm.
- **Unresolved:** best exercise dose, effects on recurrence, safe return criteria across severities, and differentiating bone stress injury. Stretching, short-foot control and resistance cannot be collapsed into strength-set equivalents.

## 9. Lower-limb bone stress injury

**BSI-G: Hoenig et al., 2025 international Delphi consensus.** [Publisher DOI/full indexed discussion](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108616); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39638438/). DOI **10.1136/bjsports-2024-108616**, PMID **39638438**. Abstract and full indexed discussion inspected; direct BMJ HTML/PDF requests intermittently returned 403; individual recommendation tables were not fully retrieved. Forty-one panel members; 28 completed final rounds; consensus on 41/58 statements. Management depends on anatomical site, injury severity and sport demands. Consensus high-risk locations: superior femoral-neck cortex, anterior tibial cortex, navicular, fifth-metatarsal base. Structured rehabilitation and interdisciplinary care; gradual return considers pain-free daily/sport activity, palpation and bone-loading tests. This is expert consensus, not a treatment-dose RCT. Failure to agree on some pain/technology recommendations must not be silently converted into universal permission or prohibition. No exercise-specific harms comparison.

**BSI-S: George et al., 2024 return-to-running scoping review.** [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393297/); [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11393297/fullTextXML). DOI **10.1007/s40279-024-02051-y**, PMID **39141251**. Full indexed text and XML inspected. Fifty sources, predominantly reviews/commentaries; recommendations largely level IV evidence. Before running, consider bony tenderness, pain-free walking, radiological healing in high-risk injury, strength/loading capacity and contributing factors. Low-risk posteromedial tibial injury differs from high-risk anterior tibial cortex; imaging healing is not routinely required for every low-risk case. Tenderness criteria conflict across studies. Individual walk/run intervals and increasing distance before speed are proposed; a universal 10% rule is not supported. Resistance/plyometric suggestions often derive from other populations or later recovery stages, not acute-fracture efficacy trials. No validated app-wide return timetable, safe impact dose or harm rate.

**BSI-R: Nattiv et al., 2013 prospective cohort.** [Primary full manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4367232/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23825184/). DOI **10.1177/0363546513490645**, PMID **23825184**. Full indexed results inspected. Among 211 collegiate track/cross-country athletes followed over five years, 34 sustained 61 injuries; 43 MRI-graded injuries contributed return data. Higher MRI grade and lower total-body BMD predicted longer recovery. Grades 3–4 returned in 23.6±2.4 weeks versus 13.1±2.0 for grades 1–2; trabecular-site injuries could take longer. These are prognostic group averages under clinical care, not clearance thresholds. No randomised exercise comparison, prescribed generic loading dose or demonstration that a particular substitute is safe during an active fracture.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Restriction-only candidate:** active/recent/suspected injury with unknown site, grade or clearance. Do not supply a self-prescribed fracture-loading or return-to-running protocol.
- **App inference:** withhold impact and other affected-limb loading outside clinician-provided limits until diagnosis/risk/stage are established; this is a conservative product boundary supported by site/stage-dependent management, not a claim that every lower-limb exercise harms every BSI.
- **Supportive exercise:** later-stage strength/balance/loading may be useful within clinical rehabilitation. No independent “safe upper-body workout” or nonimpact cardio rule is established here: transfers, standing, pedal forces and specific fracture location still matter.
- **Unresolved:** clinician's weight-bearing/activity instructions, healing/return criteria, low energy availability/bone-health contributors, and applicability across all lower-limb sites. Pain-monitoring tolerances from Achilles/MTSS cannot override BSI restrictions.

## 10. First metatarsophalangeal (MTP) osteoarthritis

**MTP-S: Munteanu et al., 2024 Cochrane review (minor amendment noted January 2025).** [Cochrane summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD007809_what-are-most-effective-treatments-arthritis-big-toe-excluding-surgery); [PMC full review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11181457/). DOI **10.1002/14651858.CD007809.pub3**. Full indexed review sections inspected; XML attempt failed (500). Six trials, 547 participants; only three sham/placebo comparisons. Moderate-certainty evidence of no clinically important benefit from arch-contouring orthoses or shoe-stiffening inserts versus sham. Other treatment evidence remains limited; no high-certainty exercise prescription. Serious adverse events not reported; common device events were foot pain. Functional hallux-limitus multimodal trials are not automatically direct evidence for established radiographic OA. Review does not prove all toe extension or forefoot loading is harmful.

**MTP-R1: Menz et al., 2016 RCT.** [Primary full publisher article](https://acrjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acr.22750). DOI **10.1002/acr.22750**. Full methods/results inspected. N=102 with first-MTP OA; prefabricated modified orthoses versus rocker-sole shoes for 12 weeks. FHSQ pain improved similarly; adjusted between-group difference 2.05 (95% CI −3.61 to 7.71). Shoes had lower adherence and more adverse events (39% versus 16%; RR 2.47, 95% CI 1.12–5.44), including new back pain. No sham/no-treatment control, so improvement does not establish efficacy of either device. Participants with recurrent falls were excluded; that exclusion alone does not prove a contraindication. Devices cannot be prescribed as a universally safe substitution for exercise.

**MTP-R2: Paterson et al., 2022 sham-controlled RCT.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35272050/); [primary full PDF](https://www.oarsijournal.com/article/S1063458422006550/pdf). DOI **10.1016/j.joca.2022.01.014**, PMID **35272050**. Full indexed PDF methods/abstract inspected. Eighty-eight adults ≥45 with symptomatic radiographic OA; contoured orthoses versus flat sham insoles whenever wearing shoes for 12 weeks; 87 completed primary endpoint. No advantage for pain (difference −0.3 NRS, 95% CI −1.2 to 0.6) or secondary outcomes; adverse events generally minor/transient. This limits interpretation of MTP-R1's uncontrolled within-group improvement. It does not establish a hard motion restriction or disease-modifying device benefit.

**MTP-R3: Shamus et al., 2004 small clinical trial.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15296364/). DOI **10.2519/jospt.2004.34.7.368**, PMID **15296364**. Abstract accessed; primary full methods not retrieved. Twenty people with functional hallux limitus, pain, limited motion and weakness; both groups received extensive multimodal therapy including toe mobilisations, stretching and marble pickup. Experimental treatment added sesamoid mobilisation, flexor-hallucis strengthening and gait work, three sessions/week for four weeks. Pain, ROM and strength favored that bundle after 12 visits. No isolated exercise effect, long-term endpoint or harms extraction; uncertain generalisability to established OA. The exercise dose beyond visit frequency is not verified.

### Evidence-to-app boundary

- **Disposition candidate:** modification/restrictions only, or unresolved supportive-exercise mapping pending clinical review. The small hallux-limitus bundle is too indirect to justify a generic OA therapeutic-strength program.
- **App inference:** reduce painful loaded great-toe extension or choose a version with less toe-extension demand, honoring an independently selected joint limit. Do not infer that all lunges, calf raises, planks or squats are prohibited; execution changes toe demand. No universal degree limit is established.
- **Cross-condition conflict:** PHP-R's towel-under-toes raise increases toe extension deliberately. A fascia-treatment candidate cannot override a first-MTP restriction. Seated/flat-foot alternatives need their own applicability review rather than inheriting the towel variant's trial outcome.
