# Elbow, wrist and hand: substantive source extraction

Draft research, accessed 2026-11-01/02. Assigned inventory: lateral elbow tendinopathy; medial elbow tendinopathy; distal biceps tendinopathy; carpal tunnel syndrome; ulnar neuropathy at the elbow; de Quervain tenosynovitis; thumb carpometacarpal osteoarthritis; finger interphalangeal osteoarthritis. This is targeted evidence gathering, not an exhaustive systematic review, clinical approval, or personal prescription. Controller-reviewed serialization is retained in elbow-hand-dossiers/ and elbow-hand-exercise-requests.json; see elbow-hand-serialization-report.md for validation and remaining gaps.

## Interpretation and access

- Treatment dose below means the study's intervention, not an app default. Missing dose, starting load, allowable pain, next-day response rule and progression remain unknown. No generic five-second progression is inferred.
- A comparative treatment benefit does not establish that another movement is harmful. Diagnostic provocation, occupational association and cadaver pressure are different evidence classes from clinical workout restrictions. Trial exclusions limit applicability; they are not universal contraindications.
- Published final articles/guidelines are distinguished from protocols, abstracts and secondary extraction. PMC/PubMed intermittently returned bot-challenge or empty pages; successful indexed full-text passages, publisher PDFs and primary abstracts are identified below. No bypass of access control was used. No inaccessible full text is described as read.
- No blanket ban on all upper-body exercise, all gripping, all wrist movement, all elbow flexion or all stretching is established by this report. Candidate modifications require symptoms, stage, side and coexisting restrictions; clinical approval remains a release gate.

## 1. Lateral elbow tendinopathy

### LET-G1 — Lucado et al., final 2022 physical therapy guideline

[Guideline PDF](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/lucado_et_al_2022_lateral_elbow_pain_and_muscle_function_impairments.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453071/). DOI 10.2519/jospt.2022.0302; PMID 36453071. Published JOSPT 52(12), CPG1–CPG111. PDF indexed full-text recommendations and exercise section inspected; direct initial fetch returned 403.

Population: lateral elbow pain/tendinopathy; exercise recommendation specifically subacute/chronic LET. Grade B supports resisted wrist-extensor exercise using isometric, concentric and/or eccentric contractions. Suggested synthesis dose: 3 sets of 15 repetitions over 6–12 weeks; load/progression determination is inadequately described. Phased return to demanding work/sport is expert-opinion grade F. Stretching alone lacks convincing durable advantage; combinations cannot isolate stretching effects. Guideline acknowledges limited specific return-to-function information. Candidate wrist-extensor loading can therefore be researched, but there is no validated app-wide kilogram ceiling, elbow-angle ceiling or mandatory eccentric-only sequence. Harms/progression cannot be assumed absent or standardized.

### LET-P1 — Peterson et al., final randomized trial, 2014

[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24634444/). DOI 10.1177/0269215514527595; PMID 24634444. Abstract inspected; publisher full text not retrieved.

120 adults with tennis elbow >3 months randomized 60/60 to daily home eccentric lowering versus concentric lifting with gradually increased load for 3 months. Evaluations at 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months. Eccentrics produced faster pain improvement (approximately 10% greater responder rate; reported NNT 10) and larger strength improvement; function and quality of life did not differ. Abstract verifies daily frequency and intervention duration, not exact set/repetition, starting weight or progression increment; those values must not be copied from a tertiary account. Harms not reported in the inspected abstract. This supports a loading candidate for chronic LET, not proof that concentric lifting is contraindicated or evidence about medial elbow disease.

### LET-S1 — Wallis et al., final Cochrane review, 2024

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11129914/). DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD013042.pub2. Final review, indexed full text inspected; search through 2024-01-31.

23 trials/1,612 adults; heterogeneous manual therapy, exercise or combinations. Against minimal/no intervention, low-certainty evidence at 4 weeks–3 months favored treatment by 0.53/10 pain points (95% CI 0.14–0.92) and 5/100 disability points (0.77–9.22). Benefits were not sustained; multimodal pooling cannot identify the best exercise or dose. Adverse events were uncertain, often mild/transient pain or bruising; no serious events reported in included reporting, but incomplete ascertainment prevents declaring universal safety. Compared with minimal treatment, event RR 3.69 had a wide CI (0.98–13.97). This moderates the older CPG's confidence: therapeutic loading is a candidate, with modest/uncertain incremental benefit rather than assured rehabilitation efficacy.

### LET-S2 — Lapner et al./Canadian Shoulder and Elbow Society, final 2022 position statement

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9503571/). DOI 10.1503/cjs.019221; PMID 36130807. Full indexed text inspected.

Exercise/strengthening versus no active treatment included 9 studies/673 randomized adults. Through 1 year, pooled pain/function outcomes were similar; recommendation did not support exercise-based physiotherapy as superior. Different comparators, inclusion criteria and multimodal grouping explain why this cannot be silently merged with LET-G1 as unanimous support. No exercise-specific harm inference follows from lack of superiority. No universal exercise prohibition is justified.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Direct support: condition-specific wrist-extensor loading candidate in subacute/chronic LET (LET-G1/P1); effectiveness certainty tempered by LET-S1/S2. Clinical inference: reduce the force/repetition/volume of an individually provoking grip or wrist-extension task and reassess; substitute only if the alternative fits all other restrictions. Unresolved: named gym-exercise bans, neutral-versus-pronated grip superiority, safe pain threshold, initial resistance, stretching-only treatment, acute traumatic onset and postoperative cases. Stretching is a separate candidate, never strength-set credit.

## 2. Medial elbow tendinopathy

### MET-S1 — See, Loo and Jaafar, final systematic review, 2026

[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41887339/). DOI 10.1016/j.ctim.2026.103364; PMID 41887339. Complementary Therapies in Medicine 98:103364, June 2026; online March 24. Indexed primary abstract inspected; full text not obtained despite a free-article label.

Search through January 2025; five clinical studies/143 patients receiving wrist flexor-pronator eccentric loading, including randomized, pre/post and comparative designs. Within-group pain/function improvements were frequent; one RCT showed between-group superiority, others did not. One uncontrolled 11-year report suggested persistence. Heterogeneity and small samples precluded pooling; authors rate overall certainty low. Abstract does not establish exact protocols, harms or a validated exercise restriction. Newer synthesis prevents claiming that no medial-specific research exists, while still leaving a weak evidence base.

### MET-P1 — Tyler et al., final 2014 uncontrolled clinical study

[Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060314/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24944855/). PMID 24944855; PMCID PMC4060314; no DOI verified. Full text successfully retrieved by HTTP once and indexed passages inspected; later PMC requests challenged.

20 patients (13 male/7 female, mean 49 years) with chronic medial epicondylosis after unsuccessful prior treatment. Added rubber-bar eccentric wrist-flexor release to stretching, ultrasound, massage and heat/ice; no control group. Abstract reports 3×15 twice daily, while discussion says daily: internal frequency inconsistency is unresolved. Mean treatment 6.1 weeks; DASH 34.7→7.9 and sport-DASH 73.9→13.2. Improvement cannot isolate eccentrics or exclude natural history/expectation. Adverse-event ascertainment is not adequately reported. The text's flexor/extensor wording in a figure caption is inconsistent with the stated wrist-flexor intervention; do not build execution solely from that caption.

### MET-R1 — Donaldson et al., final narrative review, 2014

[Review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4986646/); [publisher](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/sae.12022). DOI 10.1111/sae.12022; PMID 27582910. Final Shoulder & Elbow 2014;6(1):47–56; indexed full-text sections and publisher metadata inspected.

Review addresses medial elbow, distal biceps and triceps separately; notes sparse high-level evidence and supports conservative management before surgery in appropriate presentations. This is context/expert interpretation, not an RCT defining allowable wrist flexion/pronation, valgus load or pulling volume. Medial pain can coexist with ulnar neuropathy and ligament pathology; diagnosis-specific evidence must remain separate. No dose or universal contraindication is extracted from this source.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Candidate: isolated eccentric wrist-flexor loading with a rubber bar (MET-P1), preliminary/low certainty supported by MET-S1. Clinical inference: modify individually provoking wrist-flexion/pronation/grip tasks, without declaring these movements permanently unsafe. Lateral-elbow wrist-extensor dose and progression are **indirect**, not evidence for this diagnosis. Unresolved: optimal contraction type, dose inconsistency, elbow-angle/load thresholds, isolated stretching benefit, actual sport-specific restrictions, and whether coexisting ulnar symptoms/valgus instability change eligibility. No supported hard ban on curls, rows or all gripping.

## 3. Distal biceps tendinopathy

### DBT-S1 — Sebastiani et al., final systematic review, 2026

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13265997/). DOI 10.1016/j.xrrt.2026.100770; PMID 42305929. May 5 online; August 2026 collection. Open final review, full methods/results and tables inspected.

30 studies/614 patients: 114 tendinopathies and 500 partial tears; no level I/II comparative studies, so no meta-analysis. Both operative and nonoperative reports describe pain/function improvement, but selection strongly favors nonoperative care for tendinopathy and surgery for tears. Conservative care varies (activity modification, immobilization, strengthening); dose reporting is poor. These mixed-pathology results cannot establish a loading protocol or rupture risk for isolated tendinopathy. Tendinopathy/partial tear/complete rupture and postoperative rehabilitation must remain distinct. Low-level literature and heterogeneity, rather than evidence of equivalence, constrain comparisons.

### DBT-P1 — Holshouser and Jayaseelan, final 2020 case report

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739288/). DOI 10.3390/jfmk5030056; PMID 33467271. Full text and exercise table inspected.

One 19-year-old baseball pitcher with suspected distal biceps tendinopathy, symptoms 6 weeks, five visits over 4 weeks. Pain-free ROM, manual therapy, forearm stretching and eccentric loading progressed to concentric and sport-specific plyometrics. Narrative prescribes eccentric 3×7, daily after visit two, controlled 3-second lowering; first-visit table instead lists 3×10 with 3-lb dumbbell. Stretch 3×30 seconds; later concentric 3×10. This case-specific/table discrepancy precludes one canonical dose. Improved pain/function/strength and returned to offseason pitching; no comparator and diagnosis not imaging-confirmed. Avoiding provoking heavy lifting, power cleans and pitching was individualized treatment advice, not tested universal contraindications. Harms not systematically established.

### DBT-P2 — Lee et al., final case series

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7726399/); [primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33330179/). DOI 10.5397/cise.2018.21.4.213; PMID 33330179. Final 2018 Clinics in Shoulder and Elbow article. Indexed clinical/MRI and treatment passages inspected; direct publisher PDF returned 403.

21 patients, 12 tendinopathy and 9 partial tears. Complete ruptures were excluded and managed surgically. Initial care included a long-arm splint in forearm supination for 3 weeks, NSAIDs/physical therapy; refractory cases proceeded to ultrasound-guided injection and, for five continued failures with partial tears, surgery. No exercise-versus-control test or isolated exercise dose. Splint position is an initial treatment exposure, not a safe workout ROM threshold. Mixed pathology and escalation confound attribution; report cannot prove which movements cause rupture.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Disposition: evidence-gap/restriction-review candidate, not automatic biceps rehabilitation. Possible clinician-selected supported elbow-flexor loading/forearm ROM requires diagnosis/stage clarity and execution review (DBT-P1); no app-ready dose inferred. Sudden traumatic loss of function or suspected tear is outside the tendinopathy exercise evidence. Clinical inference: modify symptom-provoking elbow flexion/supination/heavy pulling; no validated blanket supination ban. Unresolved: isolated-tendinopathy rupture risk, exact range/load, safe eccentric introduction, stretching necessity and surgical state. Lateral-elbow evidence is **indirect**.

## 4. Carpal tunnel syndrome

### CTS-G1 — AAOS, final adopted 2024 management guideline

[Full official guideline](https://www.aaos.org/globalassets/quality-and-practice-resources/carpal-tunnel/carpal-tunnel-2024/cts-cpg.pdf). Adopted May 18, 2024; adult CTS, final PDF inspected.

AAOS rates the long-term nonoperative-treatment evidence quality **High**, while the option strength is **Limited (Downgraded)** following Evidence-to-Decision deliberation (printed p.36). The guideline states that exercise and listed modalities do not improve long-term patient-reported outcomes; evidence quality and option strength are separate axes. Its long-term endpoint should not be rewritten as proof of short-term inefficacy or harm. It does not supply a universal gym-exercise contraindication, maximum wrist angle or safe load. Exercise must not be presented as treatment for progressive/severe compression or as a substitute for evaluation. No exercise prescription is extracted from this recommendation.

### CTS-G2 — Erickson et al., final JOSPT revision 2026

[Official academy PDF](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/Hand_Pain_CTS_jospt.2026.0301_new.pdf); [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2026.0301). April 2026, JOSPT 56(4). Indexed PDF recommendations/intervention section read; initial direct fetch 403.

Grade C retains optional combined orthotic/stretching treatment for **nonsurgical mild–moderate CTS without thenar atrophy and with normal two-point discrimination**; monitor meaningful improvement. It explicitly found no new therapeutic-exercise evidence for this section. A limited selected-population option and AAOS's long-term negative finding are different questions, not proof of universal gliding efficacy. Exact stretching execution/dose needs the underlying trial; the guideline recommendation alone is insufficient. No general gym-position ceiling is given.

### CTS-P1 — Abdolrazaghi et al., final randomized trial

[Full article landing/indexed abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10035085/). DOI 10.1177/15589447211006857; PMID 33855879. Online 2021; final Hand 2023;18(2):222–229. Primary abstract/results inspected; repeated full-text access challenged.

80 mild idiopathic CTS patients completed splint+median nerve/tendon glides versus splint alone over 6 weeks. Splints at night, daytime as needed. No significant between-group difference in symptom/function scores, grip or pinch; within-group improvement cannot establish added glide benefit. AAOS evidence appendix reports exercise 3 times/day, 10 repetitions, each position held 5 seconds; dose is secondary extraction, not independently verified methods. Harms not established by the accessible abstract. This is a limited glide candidate, not default treatment for severe CTS, another nerve diagnosis, or postoperative CTS.

### CTS-P2 — Baker et al., final randomized trial, 2012

[Publisher abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003999311006952). DOI 10.1016/j.apmr.2011.08.013. 124 mild–moderate CTS volunteers randomized to four splint/stretch combinations. Publisher abstract and the original 2019 CPG extraction were inspected; full primary methods unavailable.

The [primary abstract, PMID22200381](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22200381/), independently retrieved through [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID%3A22200381&resultType=core&format=json), reports a four-week home nocturnal-splint regimen with stretches six times daily and assessment at 4, 12 and 24 weeks. It reports a 12-week CTQ-Function/DASH interaction and a 24-week clinically important functional-response advantage with cock-up splint plus lumbrical-intensive stretch. Exact effect sizes/p-values and responder denominators are not given in the abstract; exact stretch holds, repetitions, geometry, progression and primary harms remain unknown. All arms used orthoses, so no isolated stretch efficacy follows.

The [2019 CPG](https://www.hand-therapy.co.uk/_userfiles/pages/files/cts_nhs_england_leaflet.pdf), CPG50, reports 103 analyzed, no thenar atrophy/normal two-point discrimination, 12-week differences but no 3-way interaction at 4/24 weeks, and then summarizes no superior combination. It separately reports 25.5% surgery by 24 weeks without group difference and no reported adverse effects. Its summary and the primary abstract's endpoint-specific 24-week functional-response advantage remain an unresolved account discrepancy, with both attributions preserved.

### CTS-M1 — Luchetti et al., final 1998 primary pressure study

[Primary abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/S0266-7681%2898%2980009-0). DOI 10.1016/S0266-7681(98)80009-0. 39 CTS patients/12 controls; measured pressure at 1-cm intervals in relaxed/gripping hands across neutral/flexed/extended wrists. Patient pressures exceeded controls; relaxed neutral generally lower, extension often higher than flexion. Position, grip and measurement location interacted; simple universal pressure rules are inappropriate. Mechanistic study, no workout intervention, clinical follow-up or safe angle/load endpoint. Supports exploring a symptom-tolerable wrist position, not declaring all loaded wrist extension harmful.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Clinical inference: reduce or change individually provoking sustained grip or wrist positions; neutral handles are an execution option, not proven superior clinical treatment (CTS-M1). Stretch/glide candidates stay optional and selected-population only (CTS-G2/P1/P2); benefit is uncertain. Unresolved: outcome-based thresholds, load-bearing wrist-extension limits, progression, sensory stop rules and applicability to severe deficits/atrophy or postsurgery. Do not copy tendon-gliding instructions to ulnar neuropathy or describe the two as interchangeable.

## 5. Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow

### UNE-S1 — Caliandro et al., final Cochrane update, 2025

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12038884/). DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD006839.pub5; PMID 40298125. Published April 29, 2025; search through July 2022; full results/study-characteristics inspected.

In clinically mild/moderate UNE, information alone may improve work activities/night pain as much as adding night splints or nerve glides; confidence limited by small trials/methods. Review cannot identify best therapy by severity/imaging/electrophysiology. It does not prove no conservative treatment works or that education is adequate for progressive motor deficits. Exercise dose and general gym restrictions are not established. Reported surgical comparisons do not answer exercise safety.

### UNE-P1 — Svernlöv et al., final 2009 randomized study

[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19282413/). DOI 10.1177/1753193408098480; PMID 19282413. 70 mild/moderate patients received information about causes/avoiding provocation, randomized to information alone, night splint or nerve glides. Primary abstract: 57 followed at 6 months, 51 improved (89.5%), no significant group differences. Cochrane reports a smaller analyzed set; denominator/timepoint differences require preserving the source-specific account rather than merging. Night splint duration 3 months; exact glide repetition/hold dose not verified. Harms were not adequately reported. Not evidence that all exercise prevents surgery, nor that glides should be routine. Trial excluded several other upper-limb conditions; those exclusions are generalizability limits.

### UNE-P2 — Shah et al., final 2013 prospective cohort

[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23647638/); [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989882/). DOI 10.1016/j.jhsa.2013.02.039; PMID 23647638. Indexed abstract/full treatment passages inspected.

19 patients/25 extremities, mild–moderate idiopathic cubital tunnel syndrome. Activity modification plus rigid night splint at 45° elbow flexion for 3 months. At mean 2 years, 24 extremities available, 21 avoided surgery (88%); QuickDASH 29→11 and grip 32→35 kg. No control group; success defined by avoiding surgery, not confirmed reversal of nerve damage. Described well tolerated; no comprehensive harm rate. Night-splint angle is not an exercise elbow-flexion limit. Does not resolve whether splint adds benefit beyond advice, given UNE-P1.

### UNE-M1 — Gelberman et al., final 1998 cadaver experiment

[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9563378/). DOI 10.2106/00004623-199804000-00005; PMID 9563378. MRI and interstitial pressure while flexing human cadaver elbows. Tunnel/nerve area decreased toward 135°; intraneural pressure exceeded extraneural pressure at several flexed positions. No living clinical symptoms, exercise dose, longitudinal injury outcome or maximum safe angle. Mechanistic support for reducing sustained symptomatic flexion/pressure; not evidence for a universal 90° cap or no-flexion workouts.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Candidate modification: avoid the individual's symptom-provoking sustained elbow flexion or direct medial-elbow pressure, with support from advice-based treatment and mechanistic data (UNE-P1/P2/M1). Exact duration/angle/force thresholds unresolved. Nerve-glide candidate remains uncertain with no demonstrated incremental advantage in UNE-P1; cannot fill a strength split or replace referral for progressive weakness. No verified strength protocol, general stretching program or blanket ban on pulling/triceps exercise. Severity, nerve instability, trauma and surgery need separate clinical handling.

## 6. De Quervain tenosynovitis

### DQT-S1 — Challoumas et al., final 2023 systematic review/network meta-analysis

[Publisher full text](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2811119). DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.37001; PMID 37889490. Final October 27, 2023, 6(10):e2337001. Full indexed results/recommendations inspected.

30 randomized studies/1,663 participants, mean age 46, 80% female; 19 contributed quantitative analyses. Adding thumb-spica immobilization to corticosteroid injection for 3–4 weeks gave statistically significant but not clinically important functional benefits; injection-related treatments dominate evidence. Heterogeneity and low-certainty networks limit rankings. This does not establish exercise dose or prove repetitive thumb movements invariably injurious. Chronicity was inadequately addressed. Injection/splint findings are background clinical context, not an app exercise prescription; procedural harms cannot be attributed to exercise.

### DQT-G1 — Huisstede et al., final HANDGUIDE consensus, 2014

[Published article PDF](https://www.guideline.care/images-recos/A%20Multidisciplinary%20Treatment%20Guideline%20for%20de%20Quervain%20Disease.pdf). DOI 10.2522/ptj.20130069. Published Physical Therapy 94:1095–1110; PDF read. 35 participating European experts, four-round Delphi, not intervention trial.

Consensus advises individualized instruction alongside treatment. Function-level advice includes reducing repetitive thumb/wrist movement, forceful manual movement, thumb flexion and ulnar deviation. It also lists avoiding static exercises. These are expert recommendations without tested load/angle/duration thresholds, not universal contraindications. Pain-provoking Finkelstein/Eichhoff maneuvers are diagnostic provocations and may also hurt healthy individuals; not automatically therapeutic stretches. The older blanket static-exercise advice conflicts with later feasibility testing of isometrics (DQT-P1), so it must remain explicitly unresolved.

### DQT-P1 — McBain et al., final 2023 randomized feasibility trial

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10584067/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37904860/). DOI 10.1177/17589983231158499; PMID 37904860. Full exercise/adverse-event sections and indexed primary metadata inspected.

28 adults randomized after 2-week orthosis/education/pain-free-ROM phase to 25% versus 70% maximum voluntary isometric thumb extension, daily for 4 weeks. Five 20-second holds, 1-minute rests; clinician measured force and adjusted weekly. No nonexercise control. Pain/function improved over time, no group superiority; strength/ROM did not improve significantly. Two exercise-pain reports (one paused 2 days); four minor orthosis events, no serious events. Feasibility is not efficacy proof or universal safety. Pregnancy/inflammatory disease and relevant comorbidity exclusions restrict applicability, not establish contraindications. Force-meter/device calibration matters; cannot replace measured MVIC with an invented resistance level.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Clinical inference: reduce individually provoking forceful thumb grip, repetitive thumb movement or combined thumb flexion/ulnar deviation (DQT-G1). No app-ready universal avoidance angle or stretching protocol. Isometric thumb extension is a **feasibility-stage candidate**, requiring equipment/measurement and clinician review (DQT-P1); the high-load arm does not justify generic high load. Static exercise conflict is retained. No isolated evidence that an aggressive Finkelstein-type stretch is beneficial; do not add it by diagnosis label alone.

## 7–8. Shared hand-OA sources: scope must stay explicit

### HOA-G1 — Kloppenburg et al., final EULAR 2018 update

[Published guideline](https://ard.bmj.com/content/early/2018/08/27/annrheumdis-2018-213826). DOI 10.1136/annrheumdis-2018-213826. Final recommendations/full indexed text inspected.

Hand OA guidance supports education, ergonomic principles, activity pacing and assistive devices; consider exercise for pain, strength/function and mobility. Specifically notes first-CMC exercise differs from interphalangeal exercise. Thumb-base stability is relevant to thumb-base OA; do not relabel thumb-specific stability as an IP intervention. Thumb orthoses may reduce symptoms with longer use (at least 3 months in evidence); no demonstrated prevention/correction of deformity. No universal loading limit or optimal exercise dose. General hand-OA studies often mix joint distributions, limiting phenotype-specific extrapolation. Postoperative rehabilitation and inflammatory/erosive flares need separate applicability decisions.

### HOA-G2 — Kolasinski et al., final ACR/Arthritis Foundation 2019 guideline (published 2020)

[Final guideline full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11488261/). DOI 10.1002/art.41142. Indexed final recommendations inspected.

Exercise strongly recommended for hand OA; first-CMC orthoses strongly recommended, orthoses for other hand joints conditional. Insufficient evidence selects an ideal exercise type/dose. Strength of recommendation does not mean high-certainty evidence for every specific hand exercise. Does not establish that forceful gripping is always harmful, that thumb-base OA and finger-IP OA require identical programming, or that an orthosis prevents deformity. This is treatment guidance, not a prohibition against general resistance exercise.

### HOA-S1 — Kjeken et al., final 2025 systematic review of evidence through 2023

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11749855/); [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39793978/). DOI 10.1136/rmdopen-2024-004963; PMID 39793978. Final January 9, 2025; indexed full extraction inspected (direct pages intermittently challenged).

Added 65 RCTs since prior review; exercise studies heterogeneous/multimodal, with 11 new exercise trials. Pooled hand exercise versus no exercise showed low-certainty small long-term pain benefit (SMD −0.34, 95% CI −0.58 to −0.09); other functional/grip estimates very uncertain. No new exercise study reported adverse events; earlier reports had few, usually mild events. Missing reporting is not zero harm. Phenotype/subtype evidence insufficient; none of the new nonpharmacologic studies specifically recruited inflammatory OA. Review supports investigation without assigning thumb-CMC results to finger IP or a universal safe intensity.

### HOA-S2 — Østerås et al., final Cochrane review, 2017

[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6464796/). DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD010388.pub2; PMID 28141914. Full review and primary-study dose tables inspected; search through September 2015.

Small short-term pain/function/stiffness benefits with low-quality evidence; regimens, dose and comparators varied. Hennig's study-table dose: three sessions/week, 10 repetitions weeks 1–2, 12 weeks 3–4, 15 weeks 5–13. Exercises included O-sign, small/large fist, rubber-ball squeeze, band thumb abduction/extension, finger stretch. This is a secondary methods extraction; does not isolate each component. One Hennig participant withdrew with sustained pain; Østerås trial reported possibly related events. Review could not establish joint-subtype response. Exact dosing should be kept linked to its trial rather than becoming a universal hand-OA default.

### HOA-P1 — Hennig et al., final randomized trial, 2015 (online 2014)

[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24667900/). DOI 10.1136/annrheumdis-2013-204808; PMID 24667900. Primary abstract inspected; full publisher methods not retrieved (dose available via HOA-S2).

80 women with hand OA, mean age 60.8; exercise+information versus information alone for 3 months, occupational therapist instruction and eight follow-up calls. 71 followed; intention-to-treat PSFS difference 1.4/10 (95% CI 0.6–2.2), other pain/grip/function improvements. 16 versus 2 met OMERACT-OARSI response criteria. Abstract describes good tolerance, but review notes one sustained-pain withdrawal. Mixed hand-OA sample, no isolated finger-IP subgroup effect established. It supports a hand exercise candidate and adherence support, not certainty that each component is effective or safe for every joint phenotype.

### HOA-P2 — Østerås et al., final randomized trial, 2014

[Published full PDF](https://www.oarsijournal.com/article/S1063458414011595/pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25008206/). DOI 10.1016/j.joca.2014.06.036; PMID 25008206. Final full trial and adverse-event section read.

130 physician-confirmed hand-OA participants (90% women, mean age 66), 12-week group/home exercise versus usual care. Three weekly sessions; 10 repetitions initially, 15 after week 2; mixed hand/shoulder exercises. At 3 months FIHOA difference −0.5/30 (CI −1.6 to 0.6), PSFS +0.9/10 (0.1–1.7), small pain/stiffness changes; no significant group differences at 6 months. No grip/dexterity advantage. Primary report: three possible finger pain/inflammation/swelling events; five increased neck/shoulder pain reports related to shoulder exercises, one withdrawal. Shows limited durability and the need to respect other affected regions. Mixed hand OA does not provide isolated DIP/PIP effect sizes.

## 7. Thumb carpometacarpal osteoarthritis

### CMC-P1 — Wouters et al., final multicenter randomized trial, 2026 (online 2025)

[University-hosted published final PDF](https://pure.eur.nl/ws/files/214451072/1-s2.0-S1063458425011872-main.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41138847/). ## 8. Finger interphalangeal osteoarthritis

### Condition-specific applicability extraction

HOA-G1 explicitly distinguishes IP-joint exercise from CMC stability exercise. HOA-P1/P2 supply primary clinical hand-OA exercise data, but enroll mixed affected joints; their effects are **indirect for an isolated PIP/DIP diagnosis**. Neither provides a verified isolated-IP optimal strength/stretch dose. HOA-S1's low-certainty pain result and missing adverse-event reporting remain applicable limitations, not assurances of finger-joint safety. HOA-P2 specifically documents finger pain/swelling events during its mixed program.

### Primary ongoing-study gap, not efficacy evidence

[DOPS registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05980793): randomized PIP-joint denervation versus patient education+exercise is a condition-specific research lead from an indexed registry mirror. The primary registry rendered only its shell; no final outcome article or regimen was verified. It is **not** efficacy evidence or a treatment dose.

### Training implications / unresolved rules

Candidate: gentle active finger ROM (small/large fist/opening) and symptom-tolerated hand strengthening/finger stretch derived from mixed hand-OA programs (HOA-P1/P2/S2), explicitly indirect and requiring joint-specific review. General ergonomic pacing/assistive devices have guideline support (HOA-G1). Clinical inference: reduce painful sustained or high-force finger grip rather than removing all hand use. No evidence-based universal joint angle, resistance or permissible pain cutoff extracted. No proof that exercise corrects structural nodes/deformity or that forceful passive stretching is needed. Inflammatory/erosive flares, unstable joints and postoperative fingers are unresolved populations. CMC orthosis/stability evidence cannot be silently reassigned to IP OA.
